About

I learn hardware by measuring it.

I came to Georgia Tech as a transfer student in August 2026, with 84 credit hours and three work terms of engineering experience behind me. That background shapes how I study. The theory in my courses lands on circuits I have already wired, measured, and debugged.

My first term at Synapse Wireless was in fall 2024, as an electrical engineering intern. I built and tested lighting fixtures and power cables, programmed SD cards and configured MAC addresses on gateways and embedded devices, and worked from schematics that included 0–10V dimming circuits, rectifiers, and microcontrollers. Few things teach debugging faster than a unit that should work and doesn't. I learned to start with measurements instead of guesses.

The next summer I moved toward automation. I wrote Python that drove Fluke, Keithley, BK Precision, and California Instruments equipment over SCPI, which brought testing processes down from days to hours, and I built the software as reusable classes so the next test could start from existing work. In summer 2026 I worked on design verification: heat chambers, current monitoring, firmware updates across existing inventory, and fixtures I modeled in Fusion 360.

That experience changed how I approach problems. I decide what I expect to see, measure what is actually there, and document the difference. I enjoy programming, but what pulls me in is the physical side: why a circuit behaves the way it does, how a chip's architecture limits a system, and how a design becomes reliable. At Georgia Tech I want to build on that through coursework, projects, and internships in hardware and semiconductors.

AThe problem and motivation

A display is one of the most direct ways to see what a circuit is doing. I chose an LED display driver because it turns a command into something visible the moment it works, and just as visibly when it doesn't. Sending bytes over a serial connection and watching specific LEDs respond makes the link between software and physical devices concrete, and that link is where my interest in hardware sits.

Serial communication appeals to me because it is simple. Two devices agree on a speed and a frame format, and then every character becomes a message. When a display shows the wrong pattern, the fault could be in my code, the wiring, the power, or my assumption about the protocol. Learning to tell those apart is the skill I want to practice. A custom, programmable output also gives me room to grow the project from static content to animation to interaction without starting over.

BProject design and approach

The planned system has four parts. A host computer sends commands over a serial link. A microcontroller, most likely an Arduino-class board, receives and interprets them. The LED display shows the result. A power supply sized for the display feeds it, with all grounds shared. The exact display and board are not decided. I plan to visit the Georgia Tech makerspace to get advice on hardware, tools, and resources before I order or wire anything.

The firmware will read incoming bytes, check them against a simple command format, and translate each valid command into signals for the display. Four things need to be understood before I connect anything: the communication protocol, the electrical connections, the power requirements, and hardware compatibility, including logic voltage levels. Larger LED displays can draw more current than a USB port provides, so the power budget is a design decision, not an afterthought.

CThe development process

I plan to work in seven stages, testing each one before moving on.

  1. Assemble and connect the display, checking its datasheet and power ratings first.
  2. Establish serial communication and confirm that bytes sent from the host arrive intact.
  3. Write software that sends commands to the display.
  4. Display custom characters, patterns, or images.
  5. Implement animations.
  6. Explore interactive functionality.
  7. Investigate upgrading to an ESP32-S3 or Raspberry Pi.

I expect to troubleshoot at every stage. My approach is to change one thing at a time, check power and ground first, verify the serial link with a serial monitor before blaming the display code, and use a multimeter or oscilloscope when behavior doesn't match what I predicted. I will record what fails and why, because those notes will form much of the final write-up.

DEngineering skills and technical learning

This project exercises the physical side of electrical engineering. Wiring teaches me to read pinouts and make connections that hold up. Interfacing a microcontroller means understanding pins, voltage levels, and timing. Serial communication means understanding framing and baud rate. Power management means estimating current, choosing a supply, and sharing a ground. Hardware and software integration means writing code that respects what the hardware can actually do, and debugging ties all of it together.

Understanding the hardware matters as much as writing the software, because code can only do what the circuit allows. A program that is correct on paper can still fail because of a loose wire, an underpowered supply, or mismatched logic levels. Knowing the hardware lets me find those faults quickly instead of rewriting code that was never wrong.

EResults and future improvements

The intended outcome is a working display system that shows custom content, runs animations, and responds to input, with a documented path to a more capable controller. At this point no results exist, and every feature is labeled Planned. The status table above will change as work progresses, and I will add photos, serial test notes, and a demonstration when they are real.

Possible future improvements include more complex animations, interactive inputs such as buttons or sensors, a higher-resolution display, wireless communication, and ESP32-S3 or Raspberry Pi versions of the system.

This project gives me a place to apply what I am learning in a setting where I own every layer, from the wire to the command. I expect it to sharpen the debugging habits I started building at Synapse Wireless and to show me which parts of hardware design I want to pursue further.
